When gates are the right level

Gate-level modelling describes a circuit as a netlist of primitive gates. It is the level of a cell library, a post-synthesis netlist, and a small amount of glue logic where the exact gate matters. It is a poor level for a 32-bit datapath, where thousands of gates would bury the intent.

Verilog provides a fixed set of built-in primitives, so a gate-level design needs no library and no package. That makes primitives useful for teaching, for quick experiments and for models that must be independent of a technology library.

Primitive syntax

Gate instantiation
and  g1 (y, a, b);        // output first, then inputs
not  g2 (yn, a);          // one input
bufif1 g3 (y_tri, a, en); // output, data, enable
and  (y1, a, b), (y2, c, d); // two anonymous instances
  • The output comes first. This is the opposite of a module port list convention and the most common source of wiring errors.
  • The instance name is optional. Naming instances makes waveforms and hierarchical references readable; anonymous instances are legal but hard to debug.
  • One statement may contain several instances separated by commas.
  • Gate primitives can take an optional drive strength and an optional delay. Delays are the subject of the next topic.

The primitive set

Common gate primitives
PrimitiveFunctionInputs
and, nandAND, NANDTwo or more
or, norOR, NORTwo or more
xor, xnorExclusive-OR, exclusive-NORTwo or more
not, bufInverter, bufferOne input; one or more outputs
bufif1, bufif0Tri-state buffer, active-high or active-low enableData and enable
notif1, notif0Tri-state inverterData and enable

A tri-state buffer drives its data input when enabled and z when disabled. That z is what allows several buffers to share a bus: at most one may be enabled at a time, and the rest must present high impedance. If two enable at once with opposite data, the bus resolves to x.

Line by line

  • and g_and (y_and, a, b); drives y_and with a AND b. The first port is the output.
  • not g_not (y_not, a); has one input and one output. The inverter ignores b.
  • bufif1 g_tri (y_tri, a, b); drives a onto y_tri when b is 1. When b is 0 the output is z.
  • nand, nor and xor follow the same output-first pattern. Their results are the complement or exclusive forms of the basic gates.
  • The testbench instantiates the design and loops i from 0 to 3, driving {a, b} with the two bits of i. The #1 gives the zero-delay gates a clean sample point.

Strengths and multiple drivers

Primitives may specify drive strength, for example buf (strong1, weak0) (y, a);. Strengths matter in switch-level and board-level models where several drivers can contend; they are largely irrelevant to synthesizable RTL. What matters at every level is that a net with two active drivers resolves according to the net type: a plain wire with conflicting drivers becomes x, while wand and wor implement wired-AND and wired-OR resolution.

Gate-level models are synthesizable in the sense that synthesis can read a netlist, but writing a large design as primitives is not productive. Use them for leaf cells, technology models and teaching; use RTL for the design itself.

Common mistakes

  • Putting inputs before the output. and (a, b, y); drives a from b AND y. It compiles and is wrong.
  • Expecting z from an ordinary gate. and and or never produce z; only tri-state primitives and undriven nets do.
  • Enabling two tri-state drivers at once. The shared net becomes x when the data disagree. Tri-state buses need mutually exclusive enables.
  • Using an unnamed instance in a design you must debug. Waveforms show anonymous gates poorly, and hierarchical references need names.
  • Modelling a whole datapath as primitives. The netlist becomes unreadable and unmaintainable; use dataflow or behavioural code instead.

Revision points

  • Gate primitives are built in and need no library; the output port comes first.
  • and, or, xor and their complements take two or more inputs; not and buf take one.
  • bufif1 drives data when the enable is 1 and z when it is 0.
  • Multiple drivers resolve according to the net type; conflicting plain wires become x.
  • Gate level is right for leaf cells and netlists, not for large behavioural designs.
